Training Camp Report - 1985 Preseason

Training Camp Rewrote The Board: Pierce Rose, Laimbeer Fell

The league's development ledger created new stars, damaged old certainties and left several contenders entering preseason with a different roster than they thought they owned.

Nina Vale1985 Preseason - Training Camp

Training camp is usually discussed as a collection of hopeful anecdotes. This one produced a redistribution of power. Andre Drummond gained 14 overall points. Paul Pierce, Andre Iguodala, Rolando Blackman and Kurt Nimphius each gained 10. At the other end, Bill Laimbeer, Magic Johnson and Otis Birdsong lost 16 apiece. Those movements are large enough to alter roles, rotations and the assumptions behind an entire offseason.

The comparison here is deliberately narrow: last season's closing player profile against the current 1985 profile. It captures the development cycle rather than pretending every point came from one morning drill. But the practical conclusion is unchanged. These are the players their clubs now have, not the players they remember paying or protecting.

Pierce Changes Barcelona's Ceiling

Pierce moved from 143 to 153 overall while his potential rose from 161 to 176. Barcelona already had a promotion argument built around his scoring. It now has a 22-year-old whose present rating belongs among the league's established stars and whose development runway remains unusually long.

The gain is more important than a simple ten-point headline. A young wing becoming a stronger primary option reduces the number of possessions Barcelona must manufacture through committee. It also changes how opponents can defend Bernard King. Loading toward one scorer becomes harder when the second can punish the same coverage.

Iguodala's move from 140 to 150 gives Ajax a related advantage with a different shape. He does not need Pierce's shot diet to bend a game. Ajax can use his size, passing and defensive range to connect lineups around Cliff Robinson and Maurice Cheeks. His unchanged 174 potential is not a disappointment; it is evidence that the club converted possibility into present value without exhausting the ceiling.

The Big Men Who Changed Their Jobs

Drummond's leap from 114 to 128 overall was the largest in the camp group. Monaco did not merely receive a better rebounder. It received a centre capable of carrying a larger defensive workload beside Alvin Robertson, which lets the perimeter defenders take more aggressive routes to the ball. His potential also moved from 114 to 129, an unexpected reopening of a development curve that appeared closed.

Brighton's Nimphius rose from 117 to 127, with his potential climbing to 141. That turns centre depth into a tactical choice. Jack Sikma can provide the polished half-court hub while Nimphius supplies a more destructive defensive spell. Kiki Vandeweghe's seven-point rise to 171 makes the top of that roster even louder, but the less glamorous improvement behind him may be what keeps Brighton stable.

Pervis Ellison gained nine overall points for Valencia. Otis Thorpe gained eight. Neither is a novelty act, and that is precisely the attraction. Valencia can now put two improved frontcourt bodies around Isiah Thomas without asking one specialist to solve every rebounding and interior-defence problem.

Crystal Palace Won In Layers

Blackman's ten-point rise to 143 was the largest Palace gain, yet it arrived inside a broader development win. Reggie Theus edged upward to 147. Herb Williams gained seven. A club that already had Alex English did not need another player to seize the entire offence. It needed more lineups that could survive when the first action failed.

Blackman's challenge is opportunity. Improvement does not automatically produce touches on a roster with English, Theus and Darrell Griffith. Palace must prove it knows what it developed. If Blackman remains a decorative scorer receiving leftover minutes, the rating gain will appear on a page more often than it appears in a result.

The Names That Lost Ground

Laimbeer fell from 151 to 135 overall. AC Milan can still build a high-level frontcourt around Joe Barry Carroll, and Laimbeer's shooting and physicality remain useful. But a 16-point fall changes him from co-foundation to supporting piece. Milan's problem is not that Laimbeer became unplayable. It is that the old hierarchy may survive longer than the evidence supporting it.

Richmond faces a more delicate version with Magic. His overall rating dropped from 140 to 124. He still sees the floor, still rebounds his position and still gives Tom Chambers a passer who can find him early. The danger lies in asking the memory of Magic to perform work that the current version cannot. Michael Adams gained eight overall points and now has a legitimate claim on more creation.

Paris Saint-Germain absorbed the widest set of awkward outcomes. Tripucka dropped 15, Steve Nash dropped 10 and Birdsong dropped 16. Dan Majerle improved by eight, but a roster cannot automatically exchange three established losses for one younger gain. PSG's response must be structural: fewer possessions dependent on Tripucka winning a difficult matchup, more responsibility for Nash as organiser, and an honest assessment of which name still deserves which minute.

Chet Holmgren's ten-point fall for Chelsea is different because he is 23. Young players do not always develop in a straight line, and a single down cycle does not erase his length or skill. It does remove the assumption that growth will arrive on schedule. Chelsea can afford patience; it cannot afford to confuse patience with certainty.

Age Explains Some Of It, Not All Of It

The cleanest explanation for the fallers is age. Laimbeer is 26, Gervin is 31 and several members of the established class have reached the part of a career where the direction of travel becomes less friendly. That explanation is useful without being complete. Magic is 24. Tripucka is 24. Nash is 24. Holmgren is 23. Their declines cannot be filed under inevitable erosion and forgotten.

The young fallers create harder front-office decisions because a club expects time to solve them. PSG can point to Tripucka's scoring history and Nash's passing. Chelsea can point to Holmgren's defensive tools. Richmond can point to everything Magic has already proved. Those arguments deserve weight, but none identifies which lost function returns automatically. A player can remain valuable while becoming valuable in a different way.

The risers also resist one tidy explanation. Drummond reopened a ceiling that appeared settled. Pierce extended one that was already high. Nimphius turned a depth role into a possible weapon, while Blackman's gain may exceed the minutes available to express it. Development did not reward one roster strategy. It rewarded clubs prepared to notice what kind of improvement actually arrived.

The Front Offices Are On The Clock

Barcelona and Ajax have the easiest decisions: expand the responsibility of players who already occupied important roles. Monaco and Brighton must be more deliberate because their gains alter lineup combinations. Palace has to create minutes without turning a deep scoring group into a queue. Valencia should resist the temptation to treat Ellison and Thorpe as separate stories; their combined growth is what changes the frontcourt.

The clubs with fallers face the more political work. Reducing a veteran's role is simple on a depth chart and difficult in an actual rotation. Teammates remember status. Contracts preserve expectations. Coaches often wait for the old version to reappear because changing the hierarchy feels more dramatic than losing one more game. Training camp has already provided the evidence. The delay from here would be a choice.

Camp did not choose the season's winners. It changed the questions every contender must answer.

There is a reasonable objection to every camp verdict: ratings are not lineups, and lineups are not results. Correct. The purpose of this board is not to award wins before the ball goes up. It is to identify where the burden of proof moved. Barcelona, Ajax, Monaco and Brighton now have better reasons to demand more. Milan, Richmond, PSG and Chelsea have old plans that require new justification.

The preseason will expose which clubs noticed. A development gain can become a role, a mismatch and eventually a win. A decline can be managed through minutes and responsibilities. The clubs in danger are the ones that leave camp different and insist on behaving exactly the same.
