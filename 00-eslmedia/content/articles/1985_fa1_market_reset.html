After FA1, The Biggest Contracts Stayed Home And The Stars Stayed Available

Nine deals secured seven franchise incumbents, sent Aguirre to Chelsea and Bailey to City, yet left the market's four highest-rated free agents untouched.

Damon Cross1985 Offseason - After FA1

FA1 spent more than $926 million in total contract value and still left Darrell Griffith, George Gervin, Larry Bird and World B. Free sitting at the top of the available board. That is not a quiet opening round. It is a very loud round that answered a different question. The league's franchise players mostly stayed home; the open-market fight is still coming.

Real Madrid handed Sidney Moncrief the largest agreement of the round at $143.75 million over five years. Paris Saint-Germain kept Kelly Tripucka for $137.5 million. AFC Richmond retained Magic Johnson for $125 million. Aston Villa paid Larry Nance $105.66 million, Atletico Madrid paid Johnny Moore $100.63 million, Benfica kept Buck Williams for $100 million and Ajax secured Cliff Robinson for $94.35 million. Seven incumbents, seven declarations that continuity was worth removing all ambiguity.

Chelsea Made The Round's Boldest Move

Then Chelsea did what an actual free-agent shopper is supposed to do. It signed Mark Aguirre for $86 million over four years, a $21.5 million annual commitment to a 22-year-old wing rated 133 overall. Jim Paxson already gives Chelsea a 141-overall scorer on the wing. Aguirre does not patch a weakness so much as turn one position into a problem for everyone else.

There is an inconvenient detail: Chelsea's current roster still lists only one guard. Aguirre can be an excellent signing and leave the club's most obvious structural hole untouched. Both things are true. This is the best external addition of FA1 because the player is young, productive and difficult to find. It is not permission for Chelsea to ignore the backcourt in FA2.

Manchester City made the other external deal, signing James Bailey for $30.6 million over three years. Bailey is 26, rated 117 and answers a real frontcourt need at $10.2 million per season. It will not dominate the offseason montage. It may be the round's most proportionate contract: useful player, obvious role, manageable term.

The Franchise Bills Came Due

Moncrief's $28.75 million annual salary is the headline, but Real Madrid did not need a philosophical seminar. It needed its 156-overall guard back. The same applies to Richmond and Magic. Elite players are expensive because the alternative is building a clever explanation for losing one.

Tripucka's return keeps Paris from creating a 162-overall hole on the wing. Nance preserves Aston Villa's frontcourt foundation at age 23. Moore gives Atletico a 125-overall point guard through his prime. Williams stays with Benfica at 22 with room to grow, while Ajax locks in Robinson's 128 overall and 131 potential. These deals are not identical merely because they are re-signings. Tripucka and Moncrief protect present superstar production; Williams and Nance also purchase the age curve.

The counterargument is flexibility. Five-year contracts can age badly, and several of these clubs have other roster needs. Fine. But flexibility is valuable because it can eventually become a player this good. When the player is already in the building, refusing to pay him in defence of future options can become front-office performance art.

The Market FA1 Left Behind

Here is why the next round may be less polite. Griffith remains available at 25 with a 149 overall rating. Gervin is 148. Bird is 142. Free is 141. Greg Ballard and Jack Sikma lead the next positional tier. The opening round established that clubs will pay between $20 million and nearly $29 million per season for franchise-level certainty, and every agent representing the remaining stars can now point to the receipts.

Inter Milan still has $32.88 million in cap room and needs guards. AC Milan holds $30.64 million while Gervin, Bird and Allen Leavell remain available from its previous group. Brighton has $25.47 million and needs frontcourt help. Sheffield United has $21.91 million with guard and big depth to address. This is not a market waiting for money to appear. The money survived FA1.

Aston Villa's position is especially interesting. It retained Nance and still shows $26.97 million in room, but only two bigs on the current roster. Jack Sikma is the best remaining centre at 130 overall. Brighton needs the same thing. Chelsea may want to retain Sikma after spending on Aguirre. That is a bidding war with three credible motives and no obligation to remain sensible.

Who Won FA1?

Chelsea won the talent grab. Aguirre is the only major player to change clubs, and pairing him with Paxson creates immediate wing force. Real Madrid and Paris Saint-Germain won the no-drama category by keeping their best players. Manchester City won the value argument with Bailey. Aston Villa may have improved its position most quietly: Nance is secured, the room remains large and the next need is obvious.

Nobody needs to be declared a loser yet. AC Milan and Inter have not failed merely because their stars remain unsigned; their cap positions preserve genuine alternatives. But the grace period is over. Another round without resolving those choices would turn optionality into exposure.

FA1 paid the foundations. FA2 gets the bidding war.

The first round told us which clubs refused to let their centrepieces leave. The second will tell us who is willing to take a centrepiece from somebody else. Griffith, Gervin, Bird and Free are still waiting, and the teams with the most money are still shopping. Now the offseason actually becomes competitive.
